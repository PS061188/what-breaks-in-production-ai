Chapter 8 — Routing and placement errors

At a glance · Chapter 8 · Routing and placement

Content is filed under the wrong heading. On documents whose sections differ in wording (drug labels) or carry labels (shipping forms), it did not happen. On documents whose sections are decided by convention (clinical notes) it did: about one sentence in six went under a different heading from the author’s when the section tag was lost, and almost none when the tag was kept.

The one thing to remember

Whether this failure exists depends on the documents. Where the words or a label say which section a piece belongs to, the model does not misplace it. Where only a convention says, it does, and the rate depends on whose notes they are. What prevents it is the section tag carried through the merge step, not a better prompt or a confidence grade.

Experiment 8.1 — Drug labels: does content end up in the wrong box?

Eleven FDA labels carrying all three sections (indications, contraindications, adverse reactions), three boxes each: 33 placements, three live runs, the plain prompt and the chapter’s constrained prompt. The code locates each returned quotation in the sections to decide where it came from.

Result

No real placement error in any run, with either prompt. The single case the scorer reported was an artefact: the locator compared only the first 80 characters of a quote (a constant correct for Chapter 9), and those characters — the drug name and strength — open every section of a label. At 200 characters, or on the whole quote, it scores as correctly placed.

Experiment 8.2 — The model’s account of its own placement

The fuller version of the prompt asks for a placement_confidence of HIGH, MEDIUM or LOW per field and a requires_review flag. On the labels it graded 97% of fields HIGH in every run, never issued LOW, and set requires_review on 0 of 11 records; with nothing misplaced, the grade could not be tested.

Withdrawn

An earlier version of this report said the model named the wrong section for its own quotation in 18% of cases (3–15% on re-runs) and called it “the finding nobody was looking for”. That figure was produced by the same 80-character locator artefact as 8.1 and is withdrawn. The lesson that survives does not need it: ask your code where a quote is, not the model.

Experiment 8.3 — Does the placement check catch misplacement when there is some?

Because nothing was misplaced on the labels, misplacement was planted: in the recorded outputs, the value and quote of two boxes were swapped by code, every pair once — 66 misplacements with known locations, and 33 boxes left untouched.

64 of the 66 rejections named the section the quote had really come from; the two that did not are both on Amoxicillin’s boilerplate quote. The check works when there is something to check; the window trades recall on long quotes against precision on where a quote came from.

Experiment 8.4 — Drug labels with the headings removed

Each label’s three sections were split into sentences and shuffled together with no headings, so the model had to decide from the words alone. Both prompts, three draws each. No real misplacement. Every flag the scorer raised was, on reading, a correctly placed sentence whose quote the locator could not find, or the 80-character artefact again. The reason is in the documents: “indicated for the treatment of” and “contraindicated in patients with” can each belong to only one section.

Experiment 8.5 — Generated shipping documents: labels varied, then removed

Forty-eight shipping documents generated by a script with invented company names and streets, so every right answer is known. Each has an origin, a destination and a billing address. Four levels, twelve documents each; three draws per prompt.

At level 4 every prompt assumed sender-first and swapped origin and destination on every document written the other way round. Prompt 8-1’s rule — fill a field only from text carrying its keywords, otherwise null — was not followed. The graded prompt marked 104 of 108 level-4 fields MEDIUM or LOW and flagged all 36 records, but gave MEDIUM to 36 correct fields and 34 wrong ones: it could tell the document gave it nothing, not which guess was wrong.

A fourth prompt asked, per field, whether the document states the role in words. It answered “stated” on 108 of 108 fields at level 1, 105 at level 2 and 82 at level 3 (every “not stated” there on a correct field), and “not stated” on all 108 level-4 fields while still filling them.

Withdrawn

A first write-up said the plain prompt “left 6, 9 and 3 fields empty” at level 2 and that Prompt 8-1’s definitions fixed that. The judge matched company names only; those fields held correct addresses returned without the company line. Scored on company or street, every prompt filled every field at levels 1–3. Levels 1–3 were also re-recorded when level 4 was added; the numbers above are the recordings on disk.

Experiment 8.6 — Real clinical notes: the corpus the failure needs

The materials

ItemDetail
Corpus60 reference visit notes from ACI-Bench (Yim et al., Nature Scientific Data, 2023; CC BY 4.0), twenty from each of its three kinds of visit: natural doctor–patient conversation (aci), a doctor giving spoken commands to a virtual assistant (virtassist), and a doctor dictating to a scribe before and after the visit (virtscribe). Role-played or constructed encounters; notes checked and rewritten by scribes or physicians. File: data/aci_bench_notes.json.
SectionsFour divisions — subjective, objective exam, objective results, assessment and plan — assigned with the MEDIQA-Chat 2023 section tagger’s rule, which ACI-Bench’s own evaluation uses: the first heading of each kind opens the division, which runs to the next such heading. Re-implemented from its header lists; identical spans on all 207 notes of the benchmark.
TaskEach note’s sentences numbered and shuffled, headings removed; the model files every sentence under one of the four headings. 2,070 scored sentences. A sentence whose exact text appears in two divisions (46 of them, usually the opening line restated under the assessment) counts as correct under either.
PromptsHeadings only; headings with definitions (Prompt 8-2); definitions plus a confidence grade; headings with each sentence’s section tag given in the input. Three draws each, 720 calls, $1.98.
Why the vital-sign lines were not scored

In these notes the vital signs (blood pressure, pulse, temperature) sit in a block headed VITALS, placed between the review of systems and the physical exam. The section tagger only recognises the four main headings, so by its rule a VITALS block belongs to whatever main heading came before it: the history. The note’s own heading makes VITALS a section of its own, and most clinicians would file vital signs as an objective finding, under the examination. Scoring those lines would have counted a model that files blood pressure under the examination as wrong by the benchmark’s rule while most doctors would call it right — adding disagreements between rules to the error count, not model mistakes. So lines under a VITALS heading were left out of the scoring: 27 sentences in 15 of the 60 notes, against 2,070 scored. The conflict itself is exactly what the chapter is about; it is just not one to score either way.

Experiment 8.7 — What does a strict output schema guarantee?

The plain prompt on the 60 notes, one draw per condition. The validator requires every sentence exactly once and no unknown ID; on failure the model is re-asked with the error, up to twice (the loop the Instructor library automates).

ConditionInvented keysUnknown IDsFiled twiceDroppedMisplaced
No schema0028376
Schema enforced by the API00333375
Schema + Pydantic validator + re-ask0000383

13 of 60 outputs failed the validator at first; all 13 were fixed by one re-ask. The schema’s guarantee (no invented keys) was never needed; the validator loop is what kept every sentence; none of the three touched misplacement.

Instructor itself. Instructor 1.17 with the same validator and up to two re-asks, on the first ten notes (probe_instructor.py; Haiku 4.5 with thinking off, and the schema written into the prompt rather than enforced): 9 of 10 ended with a valid filing, after 16 calls. 4 of the 10 first replies failed, 3 of them because the model wrote each sentence’s text after its ID. The tenth note kept that form through both re-asks; with the text removed, its filing was complete. The validator’s error listed those entries as not in the input and never said that only the IDs were wanted. On the same ten notes the loop above, with the API’s schema, failed 1 at first and fixed it with one re-ask.

Chapter 8 verdict

Real, and document-dependent. The failure does not occur where the words or a label name the section, and does occur where only a convention does — at a rate that varies threefold between kinds of visit in one benchmark. The fix that removed it is structural: keep each piece’s section tag through the merge step, and compare the tag with where the piece was filed. Prompts reduce it partly; confidence grades do not find it.
