Chapter 4 — Classification cascade errors

At a glance · Chapter 4 · Classification cascade errors

A passage is filed under the wrong category, and every stage downstream does exactly what it was told to do with a label that was wrong. The cascade is real. On clean, well-taxonomised documents the wrong label is rare — and the chapter’s own prompt makes it more likely, not less.

What to keep, what to drop

  • KEEP — Fix 3, the per-category F1 gate. The only fix that would have caught this chapter’s own dominant error before a user complained. Costs no model calls.
  • KEEP — Fix 2’s disagreement lane. Three different systems voting matched their best member and did not beat it; the useful part is that a three-way disagreement sent 1 to 2 of 96 passages to review.
  • KEEP — Fix 4, the audit trail. alternative_considered held the true category on 10 of the 11 errors (1 of 1 opening, 9 of 10 body).
  • DROP — Confidence-gated routing as the first fix. LOW was never issued once in 192 classifications. A gate on “below HIGH” has nothing to bite on.
  • DROP — The claim that the chapter’s template improves accuracy. It scored 6 points below a one-line prompt on the harder set.
  • DROP — “200+ documents per category.” 7–12 per category caught both broken categories. Volume was not what mattered; derivable ground truth was.
  • DROP — The fix’s “100% accuracy”. The recorded run gives 100% on openings and 97% (91 of 94) on bodies — and the denominator moved because the gates hold cases back before scoring.

The one thing to remember

A passing aggregate hid the two broken categories. The chapter’s template scored 90% on body passages, and under that number how_supplied sat at F1 0.76 and description at 0.78, both below the 0.80 gate. The per-category check found both with 7 to 12 passages per category and no model call. The confidence grade could not have: 9 of the 10 body errors arrived stamped HIGH.

Where this is weakest

Every cascade percentage in this chapter has a denominator of ten or fewer. The cascade itself is real — a wrong label produced confident wrong-category output on 4 of the 10 body errors and on the single opening error — but on this input the wrong label is rare, so the rates rest on 1 and 10 cases.

Experiment 4.1 — The failure: one wrong label, and every step after it is correct

A document is misclassified early. Everything downstream then processes it correctly — for the wrong category. No stage is broken. The error is invisible at every one of them.

How it was tested

ItemDetail
The derivation A passage's true class is the label section it was cut from. Strip the header, ask which of ten sections it belongs to. The answer key is where the text actually came from — derived, never authored.
Cases 96 passages from 12 documents, 10 candidate categories.
Two variants Passages taken from the opening of a section, and from the body. The body is harder and is the honest test.
The cascade The predicted label chooses a downstream extractor, which is given an explicit "return nothing" escape hatch — so the cascade rate measures whether the next stage notices, rather than being 100% by construction.
Two design problems the author had to solve honestly

Every section restates its own title in the first three words. Without stripping the header the task is a string match and every prompt scores 100%. It is stripped.

25–29% of passages still name their own section via cross-references inside the text. That leakage rate is printed next to every accuracy figure rather than hidden.

One point below a one-line prompt on openings; six points below on body passages. A reader who adopts the template may end up with a worse classifier than the one they had.

It buys something real — an alternative_considered field, which is fix 4's input and genuinely valuable. But the chapter presents it as an accuracy improvement and it was not one here.

The failure barely reproduces

90–99% accuracy. One error out of 96 on openings, ten on bodies. Every cascade percentage in this chapter has a denominator of ten or fewer. The mechanism holds — on 4 of the 10 body errors the downstream stage answered confidently for the wrong category instead of declining — but the frequency the chapter implies does not, on structured documents with a clean taxonomy.

The four engineering fixes

Fix (book's order)ResultVerdict
1 · Confidence-gated routing caught 1 of 10 body errors;
1 of the 2 passages it queued was already correct		 Cannot work — see below
2 · Ensemble majority vote 7 of 10 body errors removed, 1% sent to review, 0 correct answers held Three prompts on one model, not an ensemble — see 4.3
3 · Per-category F1 gate fires: how_supplied 0.76, description 0.78 — under a 90% aggregate Works, and is under-sold in the chapter
4 · Classification audit trail runner-up was the true category on 10 of 11 errors Works

Fix 1 — the fourth measurement of the same thing

LOW confidence was never issued once in 192 classifications. 99% of opening and 98% of body passages came back HIGH; 10 of the 11 errors arrived stamped HIGH (1 of 1 opening, 9 of 10 body).

MEDIUM was issued three times in 192 classifications, which is far too few to say whether the grade carries information. What the numbers do establish is that the grade is almost never issued, so a "route anything below HIGH to review" gate has nothing to bite on.

One part is reliable: the model's requires_human_review flag matched its own stated rule on 100% of 192 cases. It applies the rule perfectly. The input to the rule is worthless.

And the chapter contains this contradiction in its own text: it says "a classifier cannot be trusted to police its own confidence", then makes its first fix ask the classifier to police its own confidence.

Fix 3 — the one that should lead

Aggregate accuracy of 90% hides two categories at F1 0.76 and 0.78, how_supplied and description. It is the only fix of the four that would have caught this chapter's own dominant error — description misread as how_supplied, 4 of 10 body errors — before a user complained. It costs no model calls.

The chapter's stated threshold of "200+ documents per category" is also wrong here: 7–12 per category caught both broken categories. What mattered was that ground truth was derivable from document structure, not volume.

Two bugs the author found in their own scoring

Both were the same error: scoring a component against a label some other component produced. The router-policy table scored every policy — including the "no gate" baseline — against the ensemble majority; a second function credited the confidence gate with errors the ensemble had already removed, printing "errors the router caught: 100%".

Fixing it is what turned fix 1 from looking effective into catching 1 of 10 body errors. It surfaced because two numbers in one run could not both be true.

Sampling noise exceeds two of the four fixes

Same prompt, same passages, two live runs before the one now recorded: 94/96 then 93/96. Fix 1 moves one case on the opening passages — inside that spread. This is why fixed.py replays the baseline's calls rather than re-sampling them; otherwise part of the measured difference is the sampler.

Cost

647 recorded runs, $0.85 of Haiku calls (about $1.95 across iterations).
