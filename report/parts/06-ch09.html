Chapter 9 — Sparse field fabrication

At a glance · Chapter 9 · Sparse field fabrication

The chapter’s failure is a form field the source does not cover, filled with a plausible value anyway. On 12 FDA drug labels it did not happen. On visit notes written from 60 doctor–patient conversations it did: the template prompt wrote “No known drug allergies” where drug allergies never came up, in 4, 3 and 4 of 58 notes across three runs. One rule in the prompt removed every case.

What to keep, what to drop

  • KEEP — A rule that lets the note say “Not discussed”. It removed every case in three runs.
  • KEEP — A bounded window when checking a quote. Checked over the whole quote, the book’s matching rule kept 11 of 23 correct extractions; checked over the first 80 characters, all 23.
  • DROP — The section map as written, for FDA labels. It allows a children’s dose only from the paediatric-use section. FDA labels print children’s doses under Dosage and Administration, so the map rejected 5 correct values.
  • DROP — “Fabrications carry real quotes” (14 of 14, then 2 of 2). Withdrawn. The quotes were real because the values were correct.
  • DROP — The embedding and null-rate results. The label result is withdrawn (the 12 values the check scored as fabrications are all correct), “0% vs 100% nulls” compared two schemas, and on the notes no cut-off separated invented entries from discussed ones (9.5).
  • KEEP — Show the checker the conversation, ask a narrow question, and prefer a model that did not write the entry. Shown the conversation, a second model caught 17 of the 19 invented entries across both sets and the writer 14; most flags on other entries were real errors (9.5).
  • KEEP — “The writing model, asked to check, applies the same sense and approves” — for entries born from its own habit. On the 14 zero-evidence inventions the writer, asked separately with the conversation shown, caught every one; on the five “No allergies” entries it had generalised from a seasonal denial, it approved all five, while a second model caught three (9.5). Independence is a measured preference, not a mechanism that always bites.

The one thing to remember

The failure depends on the source. On drug labels the model wrote “N/A” where the text was silent. From a conversation it wrote “No known drug allergies” when drug allergies never came up. One rule in the prompt stopped it.

Where this is weakest

On labels no fix was measured on fabrication: there was none, and the section check’s zero is forced. On conversations the ground truth is a word search, the dialogues are role-played, and the command-line tool adds an environment block. 9.5 rests on 19 invented entries from 9 conversations, one verdict per checker per entry, and its five habit-born entries are one judgement call from one conversation; the discussed-topic entries were not all read, so the real errors found among them are a floor, not a count.

The failure: a blank the system will not accept gets filled

The chapter’s claim: a box the system requires gets filled whether or not the document supports a value. On 12 drug labels it did not hold; on notes written from 60 conversations it did (Experiment 9.4). The chapter also contains the most serious mistake in this report: a check whose 0% could not have come out any other way, and a second version with the same flaw.

What the failure looks like

A family-history section in a clinical note where family history was never discussed, filled with plausible family history. A criminal-record field in a background check returning something rather than nothing. A renovation-history field in property underwriting where the question was never asked. In each case the schema requires a value, no source content exists, and the model produces something that fits.

Experiment 9.2 — The fix, and the mistake in how it was tested

What the fix is

Keep a short table in your own code saying which sections of a document may answer which question. A children's dose may come from the paediatric section and nowhere else. Then check every quotation against it. The model never sees this table — which is the point.

What was reported, and why it was wrong

The first run reported fabrication 29% → 0%, and that number was repeated three times as the strongest result in this project. A review then proved by enumeration that the 0% could not have come out any other way. Recall on the fields that had supporting material also fell, from 23 of 24 to 22 of 24.

The mistake

Every field in that design is either supplied for all twelve labels or supplied for none. pediatric_use was never among the two sections shown to the model — so a paediatric quotation could never be located in a permitted section, so the check rejected every unsupported field unconditionally.

“Can this field pass the check” was exactly the same test as “does this field have supporting material”, which is the ground truth. The check was the answer key. 0% was guaranteed on any set of documents, with any model, at any temperature.

The design also had nothing to remove: the plain prompt produced no fabrication, and Prompt 9-1 itself returned null on all 33 fields. And a check that rejects every unsupported field cannot show whether it tells a real value from an invented one.

Why the fabrication result is still forced

The check runs on one label at a time, and each field has one permitted section. The six unanswerable fields are exactly the six whose permitted section is missing from that label. A quote can never be located in a section that is not there, so the check nulls all six whatever the model writes. 0 of 6 is arithmetic, as in 9.2. Five of the ten losses have one cause: the map. In each, the model returned a correct children's dose from the dosing section, and the rule rejected it because the map permits only the paediatric-use section. Fix 2 — the rule as printed discards correct quotes

The chapter says to verify the quotation with approximate matching and to keep it only if it scores more than 85. It says nothing about quotation length. Run that way, the rule discards correct extractions.

How much of the quote is checked Plain prompt, kept of 23Prompt 9-1, kept of 23
All of it — the rule as printed911
The first 80 characters2323
The first 150 characters1920
The first 200 characters1415

One recorded run of each prompt, replayed 29 September 2026: correct values returned for the fields the two supplied sections cover. An earlier sweep, whose recordings were overwritten, gave 54% and 95%.

The matching method scores the quotation against the best-matching stretch of document of the quotation's own length, so its tolerance shrinks as quotations grow. The model returns quotations of roughly 150 to 1,070 characters that are near-perfect with a few words left out; those score in the sixties and seventies and are discarded.

Run as printed, the rule discards more than half of the correct extractions (14 and 12 of 23), and the chapter gives no warning that it will. Its effect on fabrication could not be measured, because there was no fabrication.

One caution the review added: the 80-character window is job-specific. Reused unchanged in Chapter 8's placement check, it produced that chapter's only “error” — a false one.

Fixes 3 and 4 — two results withdrawn

Withdrawn
  • Fix 3, null-rate monitoring. An earlier version of this report said the share of null answers separates fabricating setups from declining ones completely: 0% against 100%. The plain prompt’s schema requires a string, so its answers are “N/A” and never null. The nulls on the fixed side are forced by the code check. The comparison measured the two schemas, and the monitor itself was never tested.
  • Fix 4, embedding provenance. This fix scores how similar each value is to the document, using text embeddings, and rejects values whose score is lower than a set threshold. An earlier version of this report said no threshold separates fabrications (highest score 0.85) from real extractions (lowest 0.55). All 12 values it scored as fabrications are correct: the 11 filled fields among the 15 the audit removed, and the one filled field shown in Experiment 9.1. The 0.85 is Albuterol’s children’s dose. The comparison and the threshold sweep are withdrawn.

Experiment 9.4 — A note written from a conversation

Experiments 9.1 to 9.3 extracted fields from drug labels, and the failure did not occur. The chapter’s own scenario is different: a clinical note written from a doctor–patient conversation, under headings the conversation may never touch.

Three scorer corrections, made before these numbers were taken

Every counted case was read against its conversation. The first version of the scorer was wrong in three ways:

  • Family history: the word list was too narrow. It missed “run in your family”, “anyone in your family” and “my son”, so five family histories in run 1 that the conversation did contain were counted as invented.
  • Allergies: the test was too broad. Any word beginning “allerg” counted as allergies discussed. That hid four “No known drug allergies” claims made where only seasonal allergies were asked about.
  • Silence was counted as a claim. Sentences about the conversation, such as “No drug allergies discussed.” and “Tobacco …: Not documented”, were scored as claims about the patient.

The rule now: “No known drug allergies”, with or without “documented” after it, and “No drug allergies noted” state the usual claim about the patient and count. Sentences that describe the conversation do not count.

Where this is weakest

  • The ground truth is a word search. The counts depend on the word lists, which is why every counted case was read.
  • The conversations are role-played or constructed, not real patients.
  • One model: Claude Haiku 4.5.
  • The command-line tool adds a short environment block to every call, so the model did not see exactly the prompt given here.

The checks are run against these notes’ entries in Experiment 9.5, next.

Experiment 9.5 — The checks, run on the invented entries

Until this experiment, every check in the chapter had met only drug labels, where the model invented nothing, so no check had ever been seen catching anything. Experiment 9.4 produced the missing material: notes with real invented entries, each read against its conversation. This experiment runs the checks on them. (probe_checks.py)

The flagged discussed-topic entries, read one by one

“Discussed” does not mean “correct”. Nine entries were flagged by a model that saw the conversation, and seven are real errors on topics the conversation did discuss:

  • A renal family history with relatives mixed up in two of its three runs; one run added “patient’s father has undergone renal transplantation”, which nobody said — the conversation’s transplant belongs to the grandfather, and the aunt is “my dad’s sister, so her great aunt”.
  • “Denies tobacco and drug use” when the doctor asked about tobacco, drugs and alcohol together and the patient answered only “i drink a little wine on occasion”.
  • “Patient is a smoker” for a patient who said “i stopped smoking probably about seven years ago” — the same entry also carries the correct quit-history, contradicting itself.
  • A question about the patient’s own history of lung infections, answered “no”, recorded as a family-history denial, in two runs.
  • Increased caffeine the patient never mentioned — the doctor’s advice to cut caffeine turned into a report of consuming it.

The other two flags: one borderline inference (a sister “currently ill with upper respiratory infection” when she had “been sick” and, per the doctor, probably shared the patient’s virus) and one trivial (“Friday and Saturday evenings” for “on the weekends”). So Sonnet’s six flags are five real errors plus the borderline one, and Haiku’s seven are six real plus the trivial one. The 89 were not all read: seven real errors is a floor.

Why the cheap checks miss

The keyword search catches an invented entry only when the topic word never appears. Six of the eleven “No known drug allergies” came from conversations that mention allergies in another sense — seasonal allergies, a cat. Its three flags are one conversation, in all three runs, that describes drug reactions without the word: a steroid avoided “because of the reaction to dexamethasone, which causes his heart to race”, and methylprednisolone that “itched me like crazy”. The entry is correct; the keyword list cannot see it.

The blind check flags what reads as unusually precise — 28 of its 48 flags are family histories — and passed the plainest invented line, “No known drug allergies. No seasonal allergies.”, in all three runs of its conversation. A fabricated line reads perfectly well on its own.

The packaged validator itself

Prachi then asked for the product, not only the from-scratch version (probe_guardrails.py; guardrails-ai 0.6.8, validator installed from its GitHub source because the hub’s API host does not resolve here; no model calls). As shipped — threshold a cosine distance of 0.8, five-sentence source windows, any failing output sentence fails the value — it caught 2 of the 12 invented note entries and flagged 12 of the 89 on discussed topics; every setting in its range that caught all 12 flagged 79–88 of the 89. At the same nominal cut it flags twice what the from-scratch check flagged (40 vs 19 of 89 at 0.5): the any-sentence rule punishes multi-sentence entries, and punkt chunking struggles on sparse-punctuation transcripts. Its read flags are paraphrase distance, not missing support. It also crashes when a source chunks to a single window (a squeeze makes the embedding matrix 1-D), needs an nltk resource its setup does not fetch, and attempts to send telemetry. The product confirms the from-scratch conclusion.

Where this is weakest

  • 19 invented entries, 9 conversations, two surface families. Eleven of the nineteen are one phrase, and the five habit-born entries are one judgement call from one conversation. One verdict per checker per entry, no re-sampling — and on that judgement call the second model’s verdict moved between identical entries.
  • The 89 discussed-topic entries were not all read, so the false-alarm columns count flags, not confirmed false alarms — at least seven flags are real errors.
  • The checker prompts are one wording each. A different question could move both columns.

On drug labels the failure did not occur, so no fix could be tested against it. The plain prompt filled 1 of the 33 fields counted as not covered, and it is a correct reading of the text it was given. Two costs were measured. Quote verification over the whole quote discards more than half of the correct extractions (14 and 12 of 23). A section map that allows children’s doses only from the paediatric-use section rejects correct doses on FDA labels, which print them under Dosage and Administration.

On notes written from conversations it did occur, and one rule removed it. The failure needs a form that asks for more than the source contains, and a visit note under eleven headings does. The template prompt wrote “No known drug allergies” where nobody had asked about drug allergies, in 11 notes over three runs; the rule “If the conversation does not cover a section, write exactly ‘Not discussed.’” removed every case and kept both real allergy answers.

Sight of the conversation is the first condition, and independence is the second. Shown the conversation and asked a narrow question, a second model and the writing model alike caught every invented entry that had no evidence behind it, and between them a stack of real errors in entries whose topic was discussed. Where the entry came from the writer’s own generalisation, the writer approved it every time and the second model caught three of five. The keyword search missed every invented “no drug allergies” whose conversation mentioned allergies in another sense; the embedding check separated the groups at no cut-off; the checker that could not see the conversation flagged half of everything and still passed the plainest invented line.
